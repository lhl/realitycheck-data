@slowcoding @tao There is another major difference, which is that automatic theorem provers are exact. They produce a proof certificate and nothing else. In contrast, LLMs work in Natural language, meaning that in addition to a proof certificate, it produces other information that is not verified. A well known source of unverified information is definitions, specific choices of lemmas, and other concepts in the paper: we cannot be sure that the formal definitions and other concepts it produces actually correspond to anything useful or insightful (or even correct). An automatic theorem prover does not give any illusion of having generated 'insightful' definitions or concepts.

But in my opinion, there are many other important sources of 'unverified' information contained in natural language. Unlike lean proofs, Natural language proofs guide mathematicians toward thinking in a certain way. We have no way of verifying that guidance. I think despite appearances, there is a substantial amount of 'philosophical' content to natural language proofs than mathematicians like to admit. A natural language proof conveys intuitions, interpretations of results, emphasis based on importance, connections to other fields, directions for future research, the personality and way of thinking of the writer, and more, all in its structure and word choice alone. @tao (1/4)

I don’t really agree with the idea that “good problems are being consumed by AI like a non-renewable resource.”

Problems are not the whole of mathematics. An open problem is better understood as an entrance, not as mathematics itself. What truly matters is what structures we come to understand through that entrance, what mechanisms we discover, what new connections we uncover, and what new questions are generated from them.

If a problem is valuable only while it remains unsolved, and its research value rapidly disappears once an answer is found, then perhaps what is actually scarce is not mathematics, but difficulty itself.

AI is certainly flattening the difficulty landscape very quickly. But:

Flattening the difficulty landscape is not the same as flattening the mathematical landscape.

For a long time, we have often used questions like “where are people stuck?” and “how many years have mathematicians spent on this?” as proxies for importance. If AI makes that way of navigating mathematics increasingly unreliable, that only means we need better criteria for mathematical value. It does not mean mathematics itself is being depleted.

@tao (2/4)

There is also an important tension here. Whether a solution contains insight should be decided by analyzing the mathematics in the solution itself—not by looking first at who or what produced it.

A human proof is not insightful merely because a human spent years finding it, and an AI proof is not devoid of insight merely because it was found quickly.

The relevant question is:

What is actually inside the solution?

When a problem is solved unusually quickly, the more important questions should begin afterward: What mathematics appeared in the solution that we had not seen before?
