https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/117237321472650215 2026-09-08T20:32:38.602Z tao
Working out whether a question is actually worth highlighting is a lengthy, deliberate, and subjective process, often informed by historical experience on what good mathematics was generated (or not generated) while working on earlier problems of this type.  In particular, being aware of the "difficulty landscape" in a field - what questions are very easy to answer with known methods, which ones can be solved but only with some effort, and which ones are impossible - is of crucial importance in making such determinations.  And some problems only become interesting after an external connection is made.  For instance, there could hypothetically be an unexpected connection between the Riemann zeta function and the 10^10^nth digits of pi for various n... at which point the previous question of determining the 10^10^10th digit suddenly becomes relevant again.  (I should emphasize though that this specific scenario is incredibly unlikely to actually be the case; I use it only as a hypothetical iilustration.)
Every new advance in mathematics, whether it comes from technique, technology, or infrastructure (such as access to libraries of past literature) reduces the difficulty of solving problems.  This is generally a good thing; but it comes at the cost of flattening out the difficulty landscape of a field, to the point where one can no longer discern its geometry to the extent that promising questions can be extracted within the range of applicability of the tool.  Often this effect is counteracted by the ability of such a tool to enlarge the radius of the sphere of results one can plausibly reach, creating new boundaries to fruitfully explore. (2/4)

https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/117237322160500501 2026-09-08T20:32:49.098Z tao
However, one notable feature of the current AI era is the absence of any definitive such boundaries.  While AI tools have flattened the difficulty landscape now in many areas of the subject, thus destroying the ability to locate promising new problems in that area, there are no clear frontiers that are separating the "AI-feasible" problems from the "AI-hard" problems (which certainly still exist, given that the difficulty level of problems are unbounded, and can even be undecidable).  This is in part due to the rapidly changing nature of the technology, but also compounded by the refusal of AI companies to disclose their negative results, or reveal the process towards obtaining their solutions.
In fact, it is now the identification of a promising problem which is the scarce and precious resource.  We have now seen that even the rumor of someone working on a problem can trigger a massive amount of AI-powered effort to flatten it before the original research project has time to reach its full potential.  The incentives may now be pointing in the direction of no longer sharing any promising research directions with the broader community, which would reverse centuries of traditions of open science and do serious long-term damage to the future of the field. (3/4)

https://mathstodon.xyz/@tao/117237322698385581 2026-09-08T20:32:57.305Z tao
In short, the indiscriminate use of powerful solution-extraction tools can achieve the immediate short-term goal of solving problems at hand, but at the cost of sustaining the ecosystem for the next wave of progress, or in understanding the progress already obtained.
While it may be technically infeasible to completely prohibit the use of automated tools to perform indiscriminate solution extraction, I believe that we can still designate many classes of problems as being desirous of a careful analysis that not only solves the problem, but identifies insights from the solution process, and learn more about the difficulty landscape for nearby problems, and for which raw solutions without such analysis would be of negligible or even negative value for these purposes.  This is analogous to how a modern food donation drive no longer accepts arbitrary contributions even when they are verified to be technically edible, but instead maintains explicit and socially accepted standards on what level of contributions are actually sought. There is another major difference, which is that automatic theorem provers are exact. They produce a proof certificate and nothing else. In contrast, LLMs work in Natural language, meaning that in addition to a proof certificate, it produces other information that is not verified. A well known source of unverified information is definitions, specific choices of lemmas, and other concepts in the paper: we cannot be sure that the formal definitions and other concepts it produces actually correspond to anything useful or insightful (or even correct). An automatic theorem prover does not give any illusion of having generated 'insightful' definitions or concepts.
But in my opinion, there are many other important sources of 'unverified' information contained in natural language. Unlike lean proofs, Natural language proofs guide mathematicians toward thinking in a certain way. We have no way of verifying that guidance. I think despite appearances, there is a  substantial amount of 'philosophical' content to natural language proofs than mathematicians like to admit. A natural language proof conveys intuitions, interpretations of results, emphasis based on importance, connections to other fields, directions for future research, the personality and way of thinking of the writer, and more, all in its structure and word choice alone. Typically, they also convey many other things, like past related work, sources, inspirations, acknowledgements, etc,
None of this information can be verified in the same way a purely formal proof can, and as a result, an LLM produces a lot more 'noise' than an automatic theorem prover does. It is this 'noise' that is the problem.

I don’t really agree with the idea that “good problems are being consumed by AI like a non-renewable resource.”
Problems are not the whole of mathematics. An open problem is better understood as an entrance, not as mathematics itself. What truly matters is what structures we come to understand through that entrance, what mechanisms we discover, what new connections we uncover, and what new questions are generated from them.
If a problem is valuable only while it remains unsolved, and its research value rapidly disappears once an answer is found, then perhaps what is actually scarce is not mathematics, but difficulty itself.
AI is certainly flattening the difficulty landscape very quickly. But:
Flattening the difficulty landscape is not the same as flattening the mathematical landscape.
For a long time, we have often used questions like “where are people stuck?” and “how many years have mathematicians spent on this?” as proxies for importance. If AI makes that way of navigating mathematics increasingly unreliable, that only means we need better criteria for mathematical value. It does not mean mathematics itself is being depleted.

There is also an important tension here. In the fourth part of this argument, Tao explicitly says that what matters is not merely obtaining a raw solution, but extracting insight from the solution process: understanding why the problem was difficult, what new ideas appeared, and what the solution teaches us about nearby problems.
I agree with that.
But if an AI-generated solution already contains structures, mechanisms, and constructions worth investigating further, then why should we describe the event primarily as the consumption of a problem, rather than the opening of a new research entrance?
Whether a solution contains insight should be decided by analyzing the mathematics in the solution itself—not by looking first at who or what produced it.
A human proof is not insightful merely because a human spent years finding it, and an AI proof is not devoid of insight merely because it was found quickly.
The relevant question is:
What is actually inside the solution?
When a problem is solved unusually quickly, the more important questions should begin afterward: What mathematics appeared in the solution that we had not seen before? Which parts reflect genuine structure, and which are merely proof technology? Can those structures be generalized? What do they tell us about neighboring problems?

If an entrance has only just been opened and the first reaction is, “What a shame, another good problem has been consumed,” then perhaps what has really been touched is not a vulnerability of mathematics itself, but a value system that has long tied mathematical worth to difficulty, scarcity, and the privilege of being the one who solves the problem.
As obtaining answers becomes cheaper, mathematical value can move upward:
from solving a problem to understanding a structure;
from proving one theorem to explaining why an entire class of phenomena occurs;
from competing over an entrance to exploring the world behind it.
